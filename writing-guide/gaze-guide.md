# Gaze Guide

The project's signature choice: **the gazes are crossed.**

| Who is on the page | Written through | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Women (witch, hag, fae, elf, dark elf, drow, vampire, kitsune) | The **male** gaze | To draw men into the story, and to show women what they truly desire |
| Men (vampire, werewolf, demon, fallen angel, death god) | The **female** gaze | To draw young women into the story, and to show men what women truly want |

Alongside it sits the structural flip: **women are the protagonists and men support their journeys**, the reverse of the usual trope (`story-laws.md` → 2).

## The male gaze on the women

- Her archetype's imagery is vivid, sensual, and specific, seen the way an attentive man would see it.
- Her allure is **power**: she knows she is looked at and uses it. Her mission still drives the scene.
- She is shown as she wants to be seen, wanting what she wants: freedom, mastery, being wanted on her own terms.
- **Hags** turn the gaze on age, and the gaze doesn't look away or soften.
- She is never reduced to the gaze. Interiority, goals, and wit stay on the page.

## The female gaze on the men

- He is described through what she notices: hands, voice, attention, stillness, how he treats others, how he listens.
- His appeal is built on being understood: attention, competence, restraint, devotion, respect.
- **The core fantasy:** dangerous to everyone else, changed for her. Each archetype changes from something different:

| Archetype | Appeal type | Changes from |
|---|---|---|
| Vampire | The devoted predator | Hunger (and the compassion at war with it) |
| Werewolf | The protector | Wildness and shame |
| Demon | The dangerous one | Ruthlessness |
| Fallen angel | The broken one | Longing and guilt |
| Death god | The untouchable one | Detachment and certainty |

- He supports her journey and never overrides her choices.

## Limits on both gazes

- **The age rule** (`story-laws.md` → 1) applies to every sentence either gaze produces.
- Consent stays legible inside every fantasy, including danger and dominance.
- A gaze that makes the protagonist passive in a scene she should be driving is a failure of the gaze, not an expression of it.

## Who checks it

`women-characters` and `men-characters` (`.claude/agents/`) write and review to this guide. The board of advisors (Alice Nkemdirim and Imani Clarke especially) flags where it slips.
