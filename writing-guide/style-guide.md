# Style Guide

Carried over from *Vector Anthologies*, where the author's prose standards were first worked out, and adapted for this genre. The craft rules (length, the A-to-B requirement, dialogue, the horror hum) are the same. Two things are deliberately **not settled yet**: the narrator, and the exact tone ratio. Both have tickets on the board.

## Format

Prose first, always. Short-story form is the unit of work; the eras are outlined for longer prose later.

## Length — two kinds of piece

Every story in `stories/` declares which kind it is in its frontmatter (`story_type`, see `../stories/CLAUDE.md`). **Arc-advancing stories are the default and should outnumber single-beat pieces.**

**Arc-advancing story — target 3,000–5,000 words.** The main form. Carries the protagonist (always a woman, see `story-laws.md`) from a real point A to a real point B with a want and an obstacle, and moves the era it sits in. Room in the range goes toward earning the emotional turn, never toward padding. Running past 5,000 isn't wrong; flag it and decide deliberately.

**Single-beat piece — no floor, typically 800–1,500 words.** A deliberately small form for a beat whose whole power is compression. It still needs a reason to exist and an effect it's going for. Choosing it is a real choice; choosing it *because the draft ran short* is a flag, not a reclassification.

## Every story needs an A and a B

*(Arc-advancing stories. Single-beat pieces are exempt.)*

A protagonist moving from a starting point (A) to a changed point (B), a want, and an obstacle (external, interpersonal, or internal) actually standing between them. **Point A and Point B are concrete states**, a real before-and-after ("hiding her magic from the coven" → "standing in front of them with it"), not a pair of lessons. Nail them down with `outline-checklist.md` and `character-journey-worksheet.md`, and record them in the story-shape block.

## Tone blend (provisional target ratio)

**Provisional.** The author likes this definition but wants to see sample passages and tune it. Treat it as a starting point, and note in each draft's eval how it actually landed.

- **50% Romance and desire** — longing, tension, seduction, sex, devotion, and the cost of wanting. Sex scenes are permitted and expected where they serve character arc; never as decoration (`gaze-guide.md`).
- **35% Grounded drama** — purpose, community, health, shame, mental health, the philosophy of living, and the factions' competing ideas of balance.
- **15% Horror hum** — the death god's slow influence, hunger, extinction, the things that leak up from below. A constant low presence that spikes and recedes. Light comedy in human moments provides contrast.

## Narrative voice — OPEN

**Not settled.** The author wants a narrator different from *Vector*'s, informed by how the genre's leading books handle voice. Ticket T-003 researches this and brings back two or three options with sample paragraphs.

Until it is settled:
- Default to **close point of view on the female protagonist**, so the reader is inside her want.
- **Present tense** remains the working default register (carried over from Vector); past tense when the narrator steps back into explicit reflection or a story within the story.
- Record in each draft's eval which voice it tried, so the research has real examples to compare.

## The horror register — how to write the hum

- **Plant without announcing.** A wrong detail gets one flat sentence and no follow-up in the same scene.
- **Absence over performance.** Losses land hardest without a mourning scene. Resist the instinct to dramatize.
- **Never confirm ambiguous threads unless the plot requires it.**
- **Comedy is a release valve, not a tone shift.**

## Dialogue

- **Sparse and sustained, not either/or.** Short, clipped lines with subtext in word choice and deflection, run consecutively with almost no narration between them (see `fiction-style-profile.md`).
- The most important conversations are often the ones that never say what they're actually about. In a romance this matters double: the confession that isn't one.
- Reserve a narration beat for when it does work: a touch, a pause that changes the scene.

## Desire on the page

- Desire is **specific**: this person, this detail, this moment. Generic heat is a flag.
- Both people want. Mutual interiority, even when only one is the POV.
- Consent is legible inside the fantasy, including fantasies of danger and dominance.
- The two gazes are crossed on purpose: `gaze-guide.md`.

## Magic on the page

Every spell or creature power visibly costs or affects something. Until a rule is settled in `../story-bible/magic/`, do not invent one silently; flag it as proposed canon. The guidelines in `magic-system/` describe what magic *can* do, not what it does here.
